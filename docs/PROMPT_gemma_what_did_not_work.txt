We ran these experiments on earlier versions of the method, on the ten MLSP languages unless stated otherwise. Each difference is against the version of that time.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Eye-tracking dataset.} A version of our method for Romanian alone also used the eye-tracking measures of 95 readers from MultiplEYE \citep{jakobi-etal-2025-multipleye}, available for 95\% of the Romanian targets. They raised the correlation by about 0.007. This is an upper bound, because their weight was chosen on the test scores. The recordings cover only these Romanian texts and none of the other languages. We also tried to predict the total reading time from the XLM-R base embedding, which exists in every language. The embedding alone predicted it worse than word length and frequency, 0.517 against 0.626. Adding it to these two gave 0.622.
\item \textbf{LLM surprisal.} We tested the surprisal of the target in its context, read from a language model, as a third signal. With Qwen3 8B it lowered the fusion by less than 0.001 and made Filipino worse. With the average of six language models, the loss was 0.001. German needed a positive weight for surprisal, and Filipino a negative one.
\item \textbf{Fine-tuning XLM-R.} In an earlier setup without LOLO, we trained on English CompLex and tested on 2{,}827 MLSP 2024 instances. The best of 14 runs that fine-tune the whole encoder reached 0.461, against 0.551 with the encoder frozen. The best of six LoRA runs reached 0.462, against 0.515.
\item \textbf{Few-shot prompt.} We wrote a prompt with six labelled examples from the trial split of the other languages. On the trial split, it was our best single prompt, with 0.697. On the test split, as the only prompt, it lowered the fusion by 0.012 and made five languages worse. As a fourth prompt next to our three, it changed the fusion by less than 0.001.
\end{itemize}
